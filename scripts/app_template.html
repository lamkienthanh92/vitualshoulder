    Taut or slack after fixation

    GHLength (mm)Change vs. fixation

    Computed in the scapular plane (30°) with 0° rotation. Positive: the anchors are farther apart than at fixation, so the graft is taut. Negative: slack. The graft is treated as inextensible. The humeral anchor is the footprint centre, not the medial edge of the greater tuberosity where anchors are usually placed, so the absolute length may be a few millimetres longer than in practice; the trend with angle is more reliable than the absolute value.

    Compare plans

    Set a technique, fixation angle and thickness above, then add it. Each plan is scored on the same model with glenohumeral motion only. Length change is the kinematic length demand: positive means the graft would have to stretch (taut), negative means slack. It is not a force, and efficacy comes from cadaver and clinical papers, not from this simulation. In this model the length change between 10° and 30° is about 1.5 times the cadaver value of Mihata 2016, so absolute strains are probably overestimated; compare plans with each other rather than reading the percentages as real strain.

        AbductionLength (mm)Change vs. fixation

        Method: the abduction axis is the anteroposterior axis of the glenoid (GA–GP); the greater tuberosity rotates about the head centre. By default the greater tuberosity is first brought into the scapular plane (neutral rotation) and then abducted, because the rotation at scan time varies widely. The green line on the images is the graft; the dashed orange line is the greater tuberosity path from 0° to 120°. Not validated in patients; for research and education only.

        Model

        Right shoulder of a single male anatomical model (BodyParts3D), with a reduced mesh so that it runs in the browser. For teaching and illustration only. The model does not represent a specific patient or population and is not a medical device.

        Motion model

        • The humerus rotates about the head centre (sphere fitted to the head, R ≈ mm); arm at the side = 0°, forearm pointing forward at 0° rotation.
        • Scapulohumeral rhythm: after the first 30°, each further 3° of elevation adds 1° of scapular upward rotation (2:1 ratio). The scapula rotates about the acromioclavicular point; this is an approximation.
        • Muscles are stretched between their attachments: parts near the scapula follow the scapula and parts near the humerus follow the humerus (the deltoid also has a clavicular attachment). The source model does not separate the tendon of each muscle; the rotator cuff tendons are part of the muscle mass and only the long head of biceps tendon is drawn separately.
        • The long head of biceps tendon runs from the supraglenoid tubercle, wraps around a sphere of R = mm and enters the intertubercular groove.
        • Bone collision, joint capsule, ligaments, labrum and humeral head translation are not modelled.

Earlier: the Planning tab is a live dashboard (sliders for technique, fixation angle, thickness, anchors, head elevation and arm position; key outputs; length-change and fixation-angle charts; editable goals; graft size suggestion with the Mihata/Ting rule; what-if effect of the graft with published values; literature benchmarks; best-anchor search; pinned plan comparison; clinical profile; flags; printable plan that includes the dashboard). The dashboard can run on the geometry of a patient case (STL or DICOM landmarks) for single-bundle plans, and a Monte Carlo estimate shows how landmark placement error spreads the length curve.
